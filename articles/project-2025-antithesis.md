### Social Programs Decimated

Housing subsidies and mental health services were slashed, fueling a steep rise in homelessness. The number of homeless people grew in the 1980s, nearly doubling from 1984 to 1987. According to Don Mitchell, this was in part due to the neoliberal reforms of the Reagan presidency, as housing and social service cuts increased and also the economy suffered a recession early in the decade. (What Causes Homelessness? Start With Capitalism. - Bloomberg), (Mean Streets).

Between 1980 and 1989, the Department of Housing and Urban Development's (HUD) budget authority was reduced from $74 billion to $19 billion, leading to an inadequate supply of affordable housing to meet the growing demand of low-income populations. People of color, low-income families, and those with mental health struggles bore the brunt. Communities of color experience homelessness at disproportionately high rates, a consequence of structural racism and discrimination. (How homelessness impacts marginalized communities - Pallet Shelter)

Cuts in federal low-income housing programs have undermined community plans to fight homelessness, affecting approximately 3 million Americans each year, including 1 million children. (Cuts in Federal Housing Assistance Are Undermining Community Plans to End Homelessness | Center on Budget and Policy Priorities) Individuals experiencing homelessness are disproportionately likely to have mental health issues, with studies indicating high rates of traumatic brain injuries and exposure to violence among this population. (The Costs and Harms of Homelessness - Community Solutions)

**But wait, there's more: the gutting of the Mental Health Systems Act of 1980.**

The Mental Health Systems Act (MHSA) of 1980, signed by President Jimmy Carter, aimed to overhaul mental health care in the United States by prioritizing community-based care over institutionalization. This landmark legislation followed decades of deinstitutionalization and sought to bridge gaps in the system by funding comprehensive mental health centers that coordinated general health care, mental health services, and social support. The MHSA emphasized a more humane and effective treatment approach, supporting prevention, rehabilitation, and crisis intervention, and even introduced a Patients' Bill of Rights tailored for mental health patients.

However, the MHSA's impact was short-lived. Upon taking office, President Ronald Reagan, advocating for reduced federal government roles, repealed most of the MHSA's provisions through the 1981 Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act. This repeal consolidated mental health funding into block grants for states, granting them flexibility but significantly reducing overall funding. Consequently, the community mental health movement's progress stalled, exacerbating issues such as untreated mental illness and homelessness—problems that persist as significant challenges to this day. The repeal reflected a broader shift towards decentralization and austerity that has left lasting implications for America's mental health infrastructure.

I think you can imagine the snowball effect of increased homelessness plus decreased mental health care as that very snowball manifests as a wedge issue crashing into 2025.

By slashing federal funding and decentralizing responsibility for mental health care, many states were left unable or unwilling to maintain adequate community-based services. This gap in care left thousands of individuals with untreated mental illnesses on the streets, as deinstitutionalization without sufficient community support effectively criminalized mental health crises. Over decades, this underfunding snowballed, combining with other systemic issues like rising housing costs, wage stagnation, and the opioid epidemic.

In 2025, the problem is a collision of these unresolved crises—where reduced access to mental health care and inadequate housing policies perpetuates a vicious cycle of poverty, instability, and growing encampments in urban centers. This stark reality underscores the devastating consequences of neglecting systemic mental health and housing reform.

### Union Busting and Decline of Worker Power

Removing over 11,000 air traffic controllers shattered unions' bargaining power nationwide. (The 1981 PATCO Strike | UTA Libraries) Businesses found new legitimacy in opposing collective bargaining, fueling decades of wage stagnation. (PATCO Strike and the Decline of Labor | Oxford Research Encyclopedia of American History)

In 1981, President Ronald Reagan fired over 11,000 striking air traffic controllers (members of the Professional Air Traffic Controllers Organization, or PATCO) after they refused to return to work. This decisive action not only crushed PATCO but also sent a chilling message to organized labor nationwide. By breaking a federal union with such force, Reagan set a precedent that undermined collective bargaining power across industries. The strike's suppression effectively weakened unions' ability to negotiate for fair wages, benefits, and working conditions.

[Image: Union Busting Reagan (AI)]

The PATCO firings emboldened corporations to adopt aggressive anti-union strategies. Employers increasingly resisted unionization efforts, using tactics such as hiring replacement workers, outsourcing jobs, and exploiting legal loopholes to undermine organizing campaigns. This shift fueled decades of wage stagnation, diminished worker protections, and contributed to rising economic inequality.

Union membership in the U.S. dropped from 20.1% in 1983 to 10.3% in 2021, leaving workers with less bargaining power. As unions weakened, wage growth failed to keep pace with productivity gains, benefiting corporate profits at the expense of workers. Subsequent administrations and courts further chipped away at collective bargaining rights, making it harder for workers to organize. Reagan's actions against PATCO, backed by Heritage Foundation principles, created a blueprint for corporate anti-union strategies that continues to undermine worker power today.

Project 2025 risks amplifying these trends, prioritizing corporate interests over labor rights while perpetuating economic insecurity for millions of working Americans.

### Deregulation and Financial Instability

The Heritage Foundation has supported deregulatory policies, including those affecting environmental regulations. For instance, during the COVID-19 pandemic, the Trump administration, influenced by Heritage's recommendations, relaxed enforcement of certain environmental laws, raising concerns about increased pollution. (Environmental policy of the first Donald Trump administration - Wikipedia)

Heritage-backed deregulation cut federal oversight, enabling crises like the Savings & Loan meltdown. The Savings & Loan (S&L) crisis of the 1980s resulted from a combination of factors, including deregulation measures such as the Depository Institutions Deregulation and Monetary Control Act of 1980 and the Garn–St. Germain Depository Institutions Act of 1982. (Understanding the S&L Crisis of the 1980s: Causes and Consequences - Finance on Point)

These acts allowed S&Ls to engage in higher-risk activities without sufficient oversight, contributing to the crisis. While the Heritage Foundation has historically advocated for deregulation to promote economic freedom, attributing the S&L crisis solely to Heritage-backed initiatives oversimplifies the issue, as multiple policy decisions and economic factors played roles.

Deregulation remains a Heritage priority, despite repeated and demonstrable instances of financial instability that harm the broader public and it continues to advocate for deregulation, arguing that reducing government intervention fosters economic growth and benefits society. They contend that many financial crises are due to flawed government policies rather than deregulation. (The Myth of Financial Market Deregulation | The Heritage Foundation)

Deregulation leads to insufficient oversight, increasing the risk of financial instability and environmental harm. Project 2025 proposes significant rollbacks of environmental regulations, which experts warn could undermine public health protections. (Project 2025 would 'essentially eviscerate EPA,' former staff warn - The Verge)

**So what?**

Overall, the S&L Crisis of the 1980s stands as a defining moment in banking history, showing the consequences of insufficient oversight. Its effects linger in the continued call for rigorous practices, underscoring the importance of safeguarding financial institutions against potential volatility.

Perhaps its most significant legacy is the greater focus on regulatory measures designed to uphold stability in the banking system and safeguard consumer interests.

In response to the crisis, authorities tightened regulations to deter the high-risk practices that had exacerbated the problem. These changes now require banks and S&Ls to maintain more robust capital reserves and conduct more rigorous auditing, ultimately reinforcing the resilience of the financial sector.

This era also fueled debates about creating a more transparent financial environment. As a result, modern financial institutions operate under stricter scrutiny and are more focused on accountability, helping to foster a sturdier framework for consumer confidence.

## A Beast Awakened: Project 2025 in Full View

In its modern incarnation, the Heritage Foundation has chosen to go far beyond the 1980s. Project 2025 is not just a policy outline; it's a blueprint for immediate and total governmental transformation. It is divided into four primary "pillars," each carrying the potential to uproot decades of governance norms:

**Pillar I:** Consolidates a "consensus view" on governing federal agencies, with disagreements bracketed for the President to pick and choose.

- Single-Ideology Blueprint: Labels internal conservative disagreements as "brackets," providing a buffet of extreme options for a future president.
- Rubber-Stamp Governance: Cuts out moderate or nuanced viewpoints, letting ideological factions force through radical changes.

**Pillar II:** Creates a personnel database to "streamline" the appointment process, ensuring only Heritage-aligned candidates fill federal roles.

- Partisan Filter: A loyalty-based hiring pipeline that could exclude qualified career experts who do not share Heritage's worldview.
- Politicization of Agencies: Revives the Reagan-era approach of appointing ideologues over subject-matter experts, risking incompetence and corruption.

**Pillar III:** "The Presidential Administration Academy" for training (or indoctrinating) new appointees.

- Ideological Indoctrination: This is less civic education and more top-down conformity—teaching Heritage's doctrines rather than bipartisan governance.
- Groupthink Governance: Ensures any internal dissent is stifled, mirroring the anti-labor, anti-regulatory zeal that marked the Reagan years.

**Pillar IV:** A "Playbook" with agency-by-agency transition plans, ready to launch upon a new president's inauguration.

- Day One Overhaul: Proposes immediate regulatory rollbacks and policy changes, bypassing extended public debate or scrutiny.
- Shock-and-Awe Tactics: Much like Reagan's swift union-busting, but on a vastly larger scale.
